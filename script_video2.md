## MISTAKE #1: CLAIMING TOO EARLY (0:45 – 2:15)

**[CAM – Presenter leans forward, warm but serious]**

> "Mistake number one — and this is the most common one I see — is claiming Social Security too early."

**[GFX: Age timeline graphic — 62, 67, 70 with benefit amounts]**

> "Here's how it works. You can start claiming Social Security as early as age 62. But if you do that, your benefit is permanently reduced — by as much as 30% compared to waiting until your full retirement age."

**[BROLL: Calendar pages turning, clock graphic, birthday celebration imagery]**

> "Your full retirement age depends on when you were born. For most people watching this video, it's either 66 or 67. And here's the powerful part — if you wait even beyond that, until age 70, your benefit grows by 8% every single year you delay."

**[GFX: Animated comparison — Claim at 62: $1,400/mo vs Claim at 70: $2,480/mo — over 20 years difference highlighted]**

**[CAM]**

> "That difference can add up to over $100,000 over the course of a typical retirement. Now, of course, delaying isn't right for everyone — your health, your other income sources, and your personal situation all matter. But understanding this trade-off is absolutely critical before you make a decision you can't reverse."

---

## MISTAKE #2: NOT COORDINATING WITH YOUR SPOUSE (2:15 – 3:30)

**[CAM – Presenter gestures warmly, inclusive tone]**

> "Mistake number two is failing to coordinate your Social Security strategy with your spouse — and this one catches a lot of couples off guard."

**[GFX: Two-person icon, arrow showing coordination strategy]**

> "If you are married, you and your spouse each have your own Social Security benefit based on your individual work history. But here's what many people don't realize: a lower-earning spouse can collect up to 50% of the higher earner's benefit — known as the spousal benefit — even if that amount is higher than their own."

**[BROLL: Happy retired couple at kitchen table reviewing documents together]**

> "And for surviving spouses, it gets even more important. When one spouse passes away, the surviving spouse can step up to the higher of the two benefits. That means the higher-earning spouse delaying their claim can protect the survivor for decades."

**[CAM]**

> "Married couples who coordinate their timing — rather than both claiming at the same time — can often maximize their lifetime household income by tens of thousands of dollars."

---

## MISTAKE #3: IGNORING THE EARNINGS TEST (3:30 – 4:45)

**[CAM – Presenter holds up a finger, conversational warning tone]**

> "Mistake number three trips up a lot of people who are still working when they claim — and that's the earnings test."

**[GFX: Dollar bills graphic, then a portion being withheld — animated deduction visual]**

> "If you claim Social Security before your full retirement age and you are still working and earning income, the government will temporarily withhold some of your benefits if your earnings exceed a certain limit. In 2024, that limit was around $22,000 per year."

**[BROLL: Person working at a desk, paycheck graphic, calendar marking retirement date]**

> "Now here's the good news — those withheld benefits aren't lost forever. Once you reach full retirement age, your monthly benefit gets recalculated upward to account for the months that were withheld. But the temporary reduction can still cause cash flow problems if you aren't prepared."

**[CAM]**

> "The takeaway: if you plan to keep working, think very carefully about whether claiming early makes sense. In many cases, waiting is the smarter move."

---

## MISTAKE #4: FORGETTING TAXES ON BENEFITS (4:45 – 6:00)

**[CAM – Presenter nods knowingly, empathetic tone]**

> "Mistake number four surprises almost everyone the first time they hear it — Social Security benefits can be taxable."

**[GFX: Tax form graphic, percentage of benefits highlighted — 50% and 85% thresholds]**

> "If your combined income — that's your adjusted gross income, plus any non-taxable interest, plus half of your Social Security benefit — exceeds certain thresholds, up to 85% of your Social Security benefit could be subject to federal income tax."

**[BROLL: Tax documents on a desk, calculator, pen writing on forms]**

> "For a single filer, that threshold starts at $25,000 per year. For married couples filing jointly, it's $32,000. Many retirees with pension income, IRA withdrawals, or part-time work income find themselves in this situation."

**[CAM]**

> "This doesn't mean Social Security is a bad deal — far from it. But it does mean that tax planning before you claim can make a real difference. A financial planner or tax professional can help you structure your income withdrawals to minimize what you owe."

---

## MISTAKE #5: NOT CHECKING YOUR EARNINGS RECORD (6:00 – 7:15)

**[CAM – Presenter leans in, direct and practical]**

> "Mistake number five is one of the easiest to fix — and yet most people never do it. That is not checking your Social Security earnings record for errors."

**[GFX: SSA.gov website screenshot mockup, earnings history record graphic]**

> "Your Social Security benefit is calculated based on your 35 highest-earning years of work. If your employer ever failed to report your income correctly, or if there was a data entry error somewhere along the way, your benefit could be lower than it should be."

**[BROLL: Person sitting at laptop reviewing their account, close-up of a chart showing annual earnings]**

> "The Social Security Administration lets you view your complete earnings history online at ssa.gov. I strongly encourage everyone watching this video to create an account there if you haven't already and review your record."

**[CAM]**

> "Errors are more common than you'd think — especially for people who changed jobs frequently, worked for small businesses, or had self-employment income. Catching a mistake now, before you claim, gives you time to correct it and protect your full benefit."
