## SCENE 2 — WHAT IS THE 4% RULE? (0:50–2:00)

> "So what exactly is the 4% Rule? Here's the simple version: if you withdraw 4% of your retirement savings in your first year of retirement — and then adjust that amount slightly each year for inflation — your money should last at least 30 years."

> "Let me give you a concrete example. Say you've saved $500,000 for retirement. Four percent of $500,000 is $20,000. That means you could safely withdraw $20,000 in your first year — which works out to about $1,667 per month — without running out of money for 30 years, even accounting for market ups and downs."

> "On top of that, you'd still have Social Security income, possibly a pension, and any other income sources. The 4% Rule is just one piece of the puzzle — but it's a powerful starting point."

---

## SCENE 3 — WHERE DOES THE 4% RULE COME FROM? (2:00–3:15)

> "Now you might be wondering — where did this rule come from? Is someone just making this up?"

> "Great question. The 4% Rule comes from a landmark financial study called the Trinity Study, published in 1998 by three professors at Trinity University in Texas. They analyzed historical stock and bond market data going all the way back to 1926 — through the Great Depression, World War II, the stagflation of the 1970s, Black Monday in 1987, and more."

> "What they found was remarkable: a portfolio invested roughly 50% in stocks and 50% in bonds could sustain a 4% annual withdrawal rate for 30 years in almost every historical scenario they tested — even the worst ones."

> "This wasn't a guess. It was based on nearly a century of real market data. That's what gives the 4% Rule its staying power."

---

## SCENE 4 — HOW TO CALCULATE YOUR NUMBER (3:15–4:30)

> "Now here's where it gets really useful — because the 4% Rule also works in reverse. Instead of asking 'how much can I spend?' you can ask 'how much do I need to save to retire?'"

> "Here's the formula: take the annual income you want in retirement and multiply it by 25. That's your retirement savings target."

> "For example: if you want $40,000 per year from your investments in retirement, multiply $40,000 by 25 — and you get $1,000,000. That's your target."

> "Want $60,000 a year? Multiply by 25 — you need $1.5 million. Want $30,000? That's $750,000. This is sometimes called the '25x Rule' — and it's the flip side of the 4% Rule."

> "Remember — this is on top of Social Security and any other income. So if Social Security will cover $20,000 a year and you want $50,000 total, you only need your investments to produce $30,000 — which means a $750,000 portfolio could do it."

---

## SCENE 5 — DOES THE 4% RULE STILL WORK TODAY? (4:30–5:45)

> "Now — I want to be upfront with you, because you deserve the full picture. Some financial experts today say the 4% Rule may be too aggressive given current market conditions — lower expected bond returns, higher valuations, and the fact that people are living longer than they did in 1998."

> "Some advisors now suggest using 3% to 3.5% as a more conservative withdrawal rate — especially if you're retiring in your early 60s and might need your money to last 35 to 40 years instead of 30."

> "Others argue the 4% Rule is still valid because it was already stress-tested against terrible markets. The research has been updated multiple times and continues to hold up in most scenarios."

> "My take? The 4% Rule is an excellent starting point and planning tool. But think of it as a guideline, not a guarantee. Your personal situation — your health, your expenses, your other income sources — should all factor into your actual withdrawal strategy."

---

## SCENE 6 — THE SEQUENCE OF RETURNS RISK (5:45–6:45)

> "There's one major risk the 4% Rule doesn't fully protect against — and I want to make sure you know about it. It's called sequence of returns risk."

> "Here's what that means: if the stock market drops significantly in the first few years of your retirement — before your portfolio has had time to grow — it can do lasting damage that's hard to recover from. Even if the market eventually recovers, you've been withdrawing money during the downturn, which locks in those losses."

> "This is why many retirees keep one to three years of living expenses in cash or very stable investments — so they don't have to sell stocks when the market is down. It gives your portfolio time to recover."

> "It's also why some people use what's called a 'bucket strategy' — keeping short-term money in safe accounts, medium-term money in bonds, and long-term money in stocks. We'll cover that strategy in a future video."

---

## SCENE 7 — ADJUSTING YOUR WITHDRAWAL RATE (6:45–7:45)

> "One of the smartest things you can do in retirement is stay flexible with your withdrawals. The 4% Rule assumes you withdraw the same inflation-adjusted amount every year — but real life doesn't work that way."

> "In years when your portfolio grows well, you might be comfortable spending a little more. In years when the market drops, cutting back your spending by even 10% can significantly extend how long your money lasts."

> "Some financial planners call this 'dynamic withdrawal' — adjusting your spending based on how your portfolio is doing. Research shows that even small adjustments — like spending 10% less in a down market year — can dramatically reduce the risk of running out of money."

> "The bottom line: think of 4% as your starting point. Stay flexible, keep an eye on your portfolio, and be willing to adjust when needed."
