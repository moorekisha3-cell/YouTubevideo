## SCENE 2 — WHAT IS A TRADITIONAL IRA? (0:50–2:15)

> "Let's start with the Traditional IRA — which stands for Individual Retirement Account. Here's how it works."

> "With a Traditional IRA, you contribute money before you pay taxes on it. That means your contributions may be tax-deductible, which reduces your taxable income right now — this year. The money inside the account then grows tax-deferred, meaning you don't pay taxes on the growth while it's sitting in the account."

> "The catch? When you take money out in retirement — those withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. You're essentially making a deal: 'I'll pay my taxes later, when I'm retired, in exchange for a tax break today.'"

> "Traditional IRAs also have something called Required Minimum Distributions — or RMDs. Starting at age 73, the government requires you to withdraw a minimum amount each year, whether you need the money or not — and pay taxes on it."

> "For 2024, the contribution limit is $7,000 per year — or $8,000 if you're 50 or older, thanks to the catch-up contribution."

---

## SCENE 3 — WHAT IS A ROTH IRA? (2:15–3:30)

> "Now let's talk about the Roth IRA — and this is where things get really exciting for a lot of people."

> "With a Roth IRA, you contribute money you've already paid taxes on — after-tax dollars. The money grows completely tax-free. And when you take it out in retirement, you pay zero taxes on the withdrawals. Nothing. Not a penny."

> "Think about that for a moment. If you contribute $7,000 to a Roth IRA this year and it grows to $50,000 over 20 years — you pay no taxes on that $43,000 in gains. Ever."

> "Roth IRAs also have no Required Minimum Distributions during your lifetime. That means if you don't need the money, you can leave it growing tax-free for as long as you want — or pass it on to your children or grandchildren."

> "The contribution limit is the same as the Traditional IRA — $7,000 per year, or $8,000 if you're 50 or older. But there's an important catch with the Roth: income limits."

---

## SCENE 4 — INCOME LIMITS & ELIGIBILITY (3:30–4:30)

> "Here's something that trips people up: not everyone can contribute directly to a Roth IRA. The IRS limits who can use it based on income."

> "For 2024, if you're single and your income is above $161,000 — or married filing jointly with income above $240,000 — you can't contribute directly to a Roth IRA. There is a workaround called the Backdoor Roth IRA, but that's a more advanced topic for another video."

> "The Traditional IRA has no income limit for contributions — anyone with earned income can contribute. However, the tax deduction may be limited if you or your spouse also have a workplace retirement plan like a 401k."

> "So first question: check your income. If you're under the Roth limit, you likely have the option to choose. If you're over it, the Traditional IRA may be your primary IRA option — or you'll need the backdoor strategy."

---

## SCENE 5 — THE KEY QUESTION: TAX NOW OR TAX LATER? (4:30–5:45)

> "Now we get to the heart of the decision — and it really comes down to one question: do you expect to be in a higher tax bracket now, or in retirement?"

> "If you expect to be in a LOWER tax bracket in retirement — meaning you'll have less income — then it makes sense to take the deduction now with a Traditional IRA. Pay taxes when your rate is lower in retirement."

> "If you expect to be in the SAME or HIGHER tax bracket in retirement — or if you believe tax rates in general will rise in the future — then a Roth IRA wins. You pay taxes now at your current rate and never pay again."

> "For younger workers just starting their careers, Roth IRAs often make the most sense — you're in a lower tax bracket now, so paying tax today is relatively cheap. For someone in peak earning years — say, 55 to 60 — the Traditional IRA deduction might be more valuable right now."

> "But here's the honest truth: nobody knows exactly what future tax rates will be. That's why many financial advisors suggest having BOTH types of accounts — to give yourself flexibility in retirement."

---

## SCENE 6 — WITHDRAWAL RULES & FLEXIBILITY (5:45–6:45)

> "Let's talk about the rules for taking money out — because they're very different between these two accounts."

> "With a Traditional IRA, you can start taking withdrawals at age 59 and a half without penalty. Withdraw before that and you'll typically pay a 10% early withdrawal penalty plus income taxes. And again — at age 73, Required Minimum Distributions kick in whether you want them or not."

> "With a Roth IRA, you can withdraw your original contributions — the money you put in — at any time, at any age, without penalty or taxes. That's because you already paid tax on it. The earnings portion does have rules — to withdraw gains tax-free, your account needs to be at least 5 years old and you need to be 59 and a half or older."

> "This flexibility makes the Roth IRA a unique emergency option as well — though it's best used as a retirement account."

---

## SCENE 7 — WHICH ONE IS RIGHT FOR YOU? (6:45–7:45)

> "So which one should you choose? Let me give you a simple framework."

> "Choose a Roth IRA if: you're young or early in your career, you expect your income to grow significantly, you want tax-free income in retirement, you want no Required Minimum Distributions, or you want to leave a tax-free inheritance."

> "Choose a Traditional IRA if: you're in a high tax bracket right now and want the deduction today, you expect lower income in retirement, or you earn too much to contribute to a Roth."

> "And consider BOTH if you want maximum flexibility — having pre-tax money in a Traditional IRA and tax-free money in a Roth IRA gives you options to manage your tax bill strategically in retirement."

> "One more thing to remember: if your employer offers a Roth 401k option, that's a whole other tool with higher contribution limits and no income restrictions. That's a topic for another video."
