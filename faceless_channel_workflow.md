## STEP 3 — REDDIT DISCUSSION THEMES

Direct crawling of reddit.com is blocked to this tool, so these themes come from search-engine-indexed summaries of r/retirement and r/personalfinance discussion patterns:

- **Tax anxiety is real and specific.** Roughly 64% of pre-retirees say they're concerned about how taxes affect their Social Security — this validates the tax-trap content angle directly.
- **Claiming-age regret runs both directions.** Some users regret claiming at 62 (locked into a permanently reduced check), others regret waiting until 70 (missed healthy retirement years). There's no consensus — which makes it a great "it depends, here's how to think about it" educational video rather than a one-size-fits-all answer.
- **RMDs are debated even among informed users** — one thread specifically asks whether people worry too much about RMD taxes, showing the audience is engaged but not settled on the topic, which is exactly the kind of "let's break this down together" opening for content.
- **Emotional language dominates** ("regret," "wish I had known," "nobody told me") — this maps directly onto the "discover too late" framing that performs well in titles.

**Sources:** [Fool.com – Americans Worried About Social Security Taxes](https://www.fool.com/retirement/2020/07/30/americans-worried-about-social-security-taxes.aspx), general r/retirement and r/personalfinance thread summaries surfaced via web search

## STEP 6 — FULL SCRIPT

**"Claiming Social Security at 62 vs. 67 vs. 70: The Regret Nobody Warns You About"**
**Estimated runtime: 9–11 minutes**

---

### HOOK (0:00–0:15)

> "Every single day, thousands of Americans make a Social Security decision they can never take back. Some regret it within a year. Others don't realize the mistake until a decade later. And the frustrating part? Both the people who claimed early and the people who waited say they regret it — for completely opposite reasons."

**[B-ROLL]** Split screen: one retiree looking relieved opening mail, another looking regretful

### PREVIEW (0:15–0:40)

> "In this video, I'm breaking down exactly how claiming at 62, 67, and 70 actually compares — in real dollars, not just percentages — and the specific situations where each age makes sense. Stick around, because the answer isn't what most articles tell you, and by the end you'll know exactly what questions to ask before you file."

**[GFX]** Text overlay: "62 vs. 67 vs. 70 — The Real Math"

### INTRODUCTION (0:40–1:15)

> "Welcome back to Retirement Wealth 101, where we break down retirement decisions in plain English. Today's topic comes straight from what real people are asking online — and it's one of the most debated, most emotional financial decisions in retirement: when to claim Social Security."

**[B-ROLL]** Warm home office setting, host-style narration graphic

### THE PROBLEM (1:15–2:45)

> "Here's why this trips so many people up: your 'full retirement age' — the age Social Security considers 100% of your benefit — is 66 to 67 depending on your birth year. But you're allowed to claim as early as 62, or delay all the way to 70."

**[GFX]** Simple timeline graphic: 62 — 67 — 70, with benefit percentages

> "Claim at 62, and your monthly check is permanently reduced — often by around 30%. Wait until 70, and it can grow to roughly 124% of your full benefit. That's not a small gap. On a $2,000 full benefit, that's the difference between about $1,400 a month and $2,480 a month, for the rest of your life."

**[B-ROLL]** Calculator, dollar bills, Social Security card mockup (generic)

> "So why doesn't everyone just wait until 70? Because it's not that simple — and that's exactly what we're unpacking today."

### WHY THIS MATTERS (2:45–3:45)

> "This decision isn't just about the size of one check. It affects your spouse's survivor benefit, how much of your income gets taxed, and how long your other savings need to stretch. Get it right, and you build in years of extra security. Get it wrong for your situation, and you can lock in a smaller income for the rest of your life — with no do-over."

**[B-ROLL]** Couple reviewing paperwork together, concerned but engaged expressions

### MAIN TEACHING (3:45–6:30)

> "Let's look at the three ages people actually talk about."

**[GFX]** Section card: "Age 62 — The Early Claim"

> "Claiming at 62 means immediate income and more years of collecting checks. For someone with health concerns, a shorter family life expectancy, or who simply needs the money now, this can be the right call. The tradeoff is a permanently reduced check — and if you're still working, your benefit can be temporarily withheld if your income is above certain limits."

**[B-ROLL]** Retiree relaxing, enjoying early retirement — hiking, gardening

**[GFX]** Section card: "Age 67 — Full Retirement Age"

> "Claiming at your full retirement age gets you 100% of your calculated benefit, with no early-claim reduction and no earnings-test withholding. For many people, this is the balanced middle option — full benefit, without waiting the extra years."

**[B-ROLL]** Financial charts showing a balanced, level income line

**[GFX]** Section card: "Age 70 — The Maximum Delay"

> "Every year you delay past your full retirement age, your benefit grows by about 8% — guaranteed, no market required. By 70, that adds up to the largest possible check. This tends to favor people in good health with strong family longevity, or a spouse who will rely on a survivor benefit based on this record."

**[B-ROLL]** Older adult smiling with family, multi-generational gathering

> "Now here's the twist almost nobody explains clearly: this isn't only about you. If you're married, your claiming age can determine your spouse's survivor benefit later. A higher earner delaying to 70 can lock in a much larger lifetime safety net for a surviving spouse — even if that higher earner doesn't personally 'need' the larger check."

**[GFX]** Simple diagram: higher earner's benefit flowing to surviving spouse

### EXAMPLES (6:30–7:45)

> "Consider two hypothetical retirees — we'll call them Linda and Frank, a composite example for illustration only. Linda claims at 62 because she wants to travel while she's healthy and active. Frank, a widower, delays until 70 because he's in excellent health, has family members who lived into their nineties, and wants the largest possible guaranteed income for his later years."

**[B-ROLL]** Split scenes: one retiree traveling, another gardening at home in later years

> "Neither choice is wrong. Linda optimized for the years she's confident she'll enjoy. Frank optimized for protection against outliving his savings. The 'right' answer depends entirely on health, other income, and family history — not a one-size-fits-all rule."

### COMMON MISTAKES (7:45–8:45)

> "A few mistakes come up again and again. First, claiming early purely out of fear, without running the actual numbers for your own life expectancy and spending needs. Second, ignoring the spousal and survivor benefit impact entirely. Third, not accounting for how continuing to work while claiming early can temporarily reduce your check. And fourth, treating this as a purely mathematical decision when health and family history matter just as much as the dollar figures."

**[B-ROLL]** Person looking over paperwork, thoughtful expression

### SOLUTIONS (8:45–9:45)

> "Before you file, get a personalized benefit estimate directly from the Social Security Administration — not just a generic online calculator. Talk through your health outlook and family longevity honestly. If you're married, discuss the survivor benefit impact together, not separately. And bring all of this to a qualified financial advisor who can model your specific numbers — this is exactly the kind of decision where a professional's perspective is worth the conversation, since I'm not a Certified Financial Planner, licensed advisor, CPA, or attorney, and this video is educational only."

**[B-ROLL]** Advisor and retired couple reviewing a printed benefit estimate together

### SUMMARY (9:45–10:15)

> "There's no universally 'right' age to claim Social Security. Sixty-two can make sense if you need income now or have health or longevity concerns. Sixty-seven balances a full benefit without the wait. Seventy maximizes your guaranteed lifetime income and protects a surviving spouse. What matters most is making the choice on purpose — with real numbers — instead of by default."

**[GFX]** Recap graphic: 62 / 67 / 70 with one-line takeaway under each
