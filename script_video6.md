## SCENE 2 — WHAT IS A QUITCLAIM DEED? (0:50–2:15)

> "Let's start with the Quitclaim Deed, because it's the one most people have heard of — and unfortunately, the one most people misuse."

> "A Quitclaim Deed is a legal document that immediately transfers whatever ownership rights you have in a property to another person. The moment you sign it and it's recorded, you give up your rights. Right now. Today."

> "Quitclaim Deeds are fast, simple, and cheap. That's why people use them. But here's the problem: they were designed for specific situations — like adding or removing a spouse from a title, transferring property between spouses during a divorce, correcting a name error on a deed, or moving property into a trust."

> "They were NOT designed to be an estate planning tool. And when people use them that way — to pass their home to a child to avoid probate — they often create serious, costly problems they never anticipated."

---

## SCENE 3 — THE PROBLEMS WITH USING A QUITCLAIM DEED FOR ESTATE PLANNING (2:15–3:30)

> "Here's where things go wrong when people use a Quitclaim Deed to pass their home to family."

> "Problem one: you lose control immediately. The moment you sign a Quitclaim Deed transferring your home to your child, it's their home — not yours. If you want to sell, refinance, or change your mind, you need their permission. And if your relationship changes — divorce, financial trouble, or a lawsuit — your home could be at risk."

> "Problem two: the Medicaid penalty. If you ever need Medicaid to cover nursing home care, giving away your home through a Quitclaim Deed is treated as an asset transfer. Medicaid has a five-year look-back period. If you transferred the home within five years before applying, you could face a significant penalty period — meaning Medicaid won't pay, and you're responsible for the costs."

> "Problem three: the capital gains tax trap. When your child receives your home through a Quitclaim Deed, they inherit your original cost basis. If you paid $80,000 for the home and it's worth $350,000 when they sell it — they could owe capital gains taxes on $270,000 in profit. That's a bill that could reach $40,000 or more."

---

## SCENE 4 — WHAT IS A LADYBIRD DEED? (3:30–4:45)

> "Now let's look at the Ladybird Deed — the Enhanced Life Estate Deed — and why it solves every one of those problems."

> "With a Ladybird Deed, you keep full ownership and control of your home for your entire life. You can sell it, refinance it, rent it, or even revoke the deed entirely — all without anyone else's permission. Your child or beneficiary has no rights to the property while you're alive. None."

> "When you pass away, the property automatically transfers to your named beneficiaries — outside of probate. No court. No delays. No fees. They file a death certificate with the county and the home is theirs."

> "And here's the key difference from a Quitclaim Deed: because you retained ownership your entire life, there is no asset transfer while you're alive. Medicaid cannot count it as a gift. And when your beneficiaries inherit, they receive a stepped-up cost basis — meaning if the home is worth $350,000 when you pass, that becomes their starting value. They sell it, they owe nothing in capital gains."

---

## SCENE 5 — SIDE-BY-SIDE COMPARISON (4:45–5:45)

> "Let me put this side by side so the difference is crystal clear."

> "Control: With a Quitclaim Deed, you lose control the day you sign. With a Ladybird Deed, you keep full control for life."

> "Probate: A Quitclaim Deed doesn't automatically avoid probate — if the recipient dies before you, the property could still go through probate. A Ladybird Deed completely bypasses probate, every time."

> "Medicaid: A Quitclaim Deed triggers the five-year look-back rule — it can make you ineligible for Medicaid. A Ladybird Deed is Medicaid-safe — no asset transfer occurs while you're alive."

> "Taxes: A Quitclaim Deed passes your original cost basis — potentially creating a large capital gains bill. A Ladybird Deed gives your heirs a stepped-up basis — often resulting in zero capital gains tax."

> "The bottom line: for estate planning purposes, a Ladybird Deed is almost always the superior choice."

---

## SCENE 6 — WHEN IS A QUITCLAIM DEED APPROPRIATE? (5:45–6:30)

> "Now — I don't want to completely dismiss the Quitclaim Deed. It does have its place. Just not in estate planning."

> "A Quitclaim Deed makes sense when: you're adding or removing a spouse from a title — perhaps after marriage or divorce. When you're correcting a name error or technical defect on an existing deed. When you're transferring property into your own revocable living trust. Or when you're simply reorganizing ownership between co-owners who already trust each other completely and understand the implications."

> "In those specific situations — fast, simple, technical transfers — a Quitclaim Deed works perfectly. Just don't use it as a substitute for a real estate planning strategy."

---

## SCENE 7 — WHICH ONE IS RIGHT FOR YOU? (6:30–7:30)

> "So which one should you use? Here's the simple framework."

> "If you want to pass your home to family at death, avoid probate, protect yourself from Medicaid clawback, and preserve your family's tax advantage — you want a Ladybird Deed. Talk to an estate planning attorney in one of the five states where it's recognized: Florida, Michigan, Texas, Vermont, or West Virginia."

> "If you live outside those five states, ask about a Transfer on Death Deed or a Revocable Living Trust — both can accomplish similar goals."

> "If you simply need to correct a title, add a spouse, or complete a straightforward technical transfer — a Quitclaim Deed is fast and affordable for that purpose."

> "And whatever you do — please work with a licensed real estate or estate planning attorney before signing any deed. A mistake on a property deed can take years and thousands of dollars to fix."
